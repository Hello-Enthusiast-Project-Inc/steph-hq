💜 What is MET?

Active patient participation · Controlled direction · Specific load · Evidence-based manual therapy

Definition

Muscle Energy Technique (MET) is a form of manual therapy in which the patient actively uses their muscles in a precisely controlled direction and at a specific intensity — against a clinician-applied counterforce — to achieve a therapeutic effect. Originally developed by Fred Mitchell Sr. (DO) in the 1950s.

Core Principles
Patient-activeClient generates the muscle contraction — clinician provides counterforce, not movement
Direction-specificContraction is directed toward or away from the barrier — not a general effort
Controlled intensitySubmaximal effort (20–30% max) — not a strength exercise; precision over force
Barrier conceptTissue is taken to the first point of resistance (restrictive barrier) before the technique begins
EffectReduces muscle tone, improves ROM, restores joint mobility, normalises reflexes — depending on technique used
The Restrictive Barrier
Anatomical barrierAbsolute end of available ROM — bony/structural; should NOT be reached during MET
Physiological barrierActive end of ROM — controlled by muscle; normal limit during movement
Restrictive barrierPathological resistance within physiological range — where MET is applied; the "bind"
Feather-edgeGentlest form — tissue taken to just before the restrictive barrier; used in acute/sensitive cases
💡 Key concept: Always work at the restrictive barrier, not the anatomical end of range. If you push past it, you cause pain and lose the neurological window.

🔵 Types of MET

Four main mechanisms — each uses a different neurophysiological pathway

Post-Isometric Relaxation
PIR
Mechanism: After isometric contraction, the Golgi tendon organ fires → autogenic inhibition → muscle relaxes
  • Most commonly used MET technique
  • Contract the tight muscle isometrically
  • On relaxation, move into new range (the "gain")
  • Best for: hypertonic muscles, chronic tightness, spasm
  • Works via GTO (Golgi Tendon Organ) → Ib afferents
  • Effect: immediate reduction in resting tone
Reciprocal Inhibition
RI
Mechanism: Contraction of the agonist → Ia reciprocal inhibition of antagonist → antagonist relaxes
  • Contract the antagonist of the tight muscle
  • Tight muscle is inhibited neurologically
  • Best for: acute pain, recent injury, post-surgical (avoids contracting the problem muscle)
  • Works via Ia afferents from muscle spindles
  • Gentler than PIR — good for sensitive/acute presentations
  • Example: tight hip flexors → activate glutes (antagonist)
Post-Facilitation Stretch
PFS
Mechanism: Maximal isometric contraction followed by rapid passive stretch during the refractory period
  • Client contracts at near-maximal effort (80–100%)
  • Immediately followed by a quick passive stretch
  • Best for: chronic fibrotic tightness, long-standing restrictions
  • Exploits the post-contraction refractory window (~2–3 sec)
  • More aggressive than PIR — use when PIR has plateaued
  • Not suitable for acute or irritable tissues
Isotonic Eccentric MET
Eccentric MET
Mechanism: Clinician overcomes patient contraction → eccentric lengthening under load → lengthens and strengthens simultaneously
  • Patient contracts; clinician slowly overcomes the effort
  • Muscle lengthens against resistance (eccentric)
  • Best for: muscle that is both tight AND weak
  • Particularly useful: hamstrings, hip flexors, cervical paraspinals
  • Improves flexibility and builds eccentric strength
  • Avoid in very acute or irritable muscles
Isolytic Contraction
Isolytic
Mechanism: Clinician forcibly overcomes maximal contraction — intentional breaking of adhesions/fibrosis
  • Most aggressive MET — use with experience
  • Client resists maximally; clinician forces through
  • Breaks up fibrous adhesions in chronic restrictions
  • Typically causes mild soreness (pre-warn client)
  • Best for: chronic fibrotic tissue, scar tissue, organised adhesions
  • NOT for acute, hypermobile, osteoporotic, or inflamed tissue
Joint MET (Articulatory)
Joint / SI / Rib
Mechanism: Muscle contraction creates a corrective force on a restricted or malpositioned joint
  • Used for: SI joint, rib restrictions, pubic symphysis, atlanto-axial
  • Patient contracts specific muscles to "self-correct" joint position
  • Indirect approach — less provocative than HVLA
  • Common: anterior iliac rotation, SI dysfunction, elevated/depressed rib
  • Preferred over manipulation when HVLA is contraindicated
  • Evidence-based for SI joint + sacral dysfunction

🟢 Standard MET Protocol — Step by Step

PIR template — the most common clinical application

PIR — Post-Isometric Relaxation Protocol
1
Position at barrier: Passively move the tissue to the first point of resistance (restrictive barrier). Do not force past it. This is your starting position.
2
Instruct the client: "Gently push / pull against my hand" — direct contraction toward the restriction (or away if using RI). Use clear, consistent language; avoid "hard" or "really push."
3
Isometric contraction: Client contracts at 20–30% maximum effort against your unyielding counterforce. No movement occurs. Hold 7–10 seconds.
4
Release: "Completely relax." Wait 2–3 seconds for full neurological relaxation (GTO refractory window). Do not rush this step.
5
Take up the slack: Gently move into the new available range — do not force or bounce. Move only to the new restrictive barrier. This is the "gain."
6
Repeat: Perform 3–5 repetitions from each new barrier. Each rep should yield additional range. Stop when no further gain is obtained.
7
Reassess: Recheck active and passive ROM. Compare bilaterally. A good outcome = 10–15° improvement and reduced end-feel resistance.
⭐ Clinical pearl: The 2–3 second post-contraction pause is the most commonly skipped step — and the most important. The GTO needs that window to fully inhibit the muscle. Rush it and you lose half the effect.
RI — Reciprocal Inhibition Protocol (Acute/Sensitive)
1
Position at barrier: Same as PIR — take tissue to first point of resistance (tight muscle is on stretch).
2
Instruct contraction of the OPPOSITE muscle: "Push away from me" (instead of toward) — contracting the antagonist of the tight muscle.
3
Hold 7–10 sec at same 20–30% effort → relax → take up the new range. Repeat 3–5 times.
4
When to choose RI over PIR: Acute injury, inflammation, muscle spasm too painful to contract, post-surgical, or highly sensitive presentation.

🩵 Regional Applications

Common presentations with muscle targets, technique choice, and position

C0 — Occiput-Atlas (OA Joint)
DysfunctionTechniqueDirection / BarrierPosition / Setup
OA Flexion Restriction (most common) Joint MET Cranio-cervical flexion (nodding) to barrier; resist extension Supine; clinician cups occiput, fingers at C1-2, localise OA only — no mid-cervical movement. Client does gentle "yes" nod (chin tuck) against clinician’s cupped hand. 5–10% effort only. Relax → take further into flexion. 3–5 reps.
OA Extension Restriction Joint MET Cranio-cervical extension (slight chin lift) to barrier; resist flexion Supine; OA taken into slight extension barrier. Client nods chin down against hand. Relax → extend slightly further. Less common — usually post-whiplash.
OA Side-Bend Restriction (right or left) Joint MET Contralateral SB of occiput on atlas; resist SB back to restriction side Supine; clinician contacts occiput, OA SB to barrier (opposite to restricted side). Client pushes head toward restriction side (SB toward clinician’s hand). Relax → SB further away. Note: OA SB and rotation are OPPOSITE — SB right = rotation left.
Suboccipital Release (general tension) PIR Cranio-cervical flexion; resist suboccipital extension Supine; clinician’s fingertips under occiput at hairline. Client gently presses head back into hands (suboccipital extension × 7 sec). Relax → allow head to drop into flexion. Do NOT use for instability or alar laxity.
⭐ C0 key rules: OA coupling = SB and rotation OPPOSITE directions (unlike C2–C7). Use absolute minimal force (5–10%). Always rule out alar ligament laxity before OA MET (Sharp-Purser, alar stress tests). VBI screen mandatory. C0 work is contraindicated with: RA (atlantoaxial instability), Down syndrome, os odontoideum, recent whiplash grade 3+.
C1–C2 — Atlas-Axis (AA Joint)
DysfunctionTechniqueDirectionPosition / Setup
AA Rotation Restriction (most common C1-2 dysfunction) Joint MET Rotation to barrier at C1-2; resist return to midline Supine; cervical spine in neutral or slight flexion to lock out C2–C7 (full flex locks AA). Clinician holds head at ears, rotates to AA barrier. Client rotates back toward midline against clinician. Relax → rotate further. AA provides ~50% of cervical rotation.
AA Flexion (atlantoaxial gap) PIR Cervical flexion isolating AA; resist extension Supine; slight cervical flexion. Client extends gently against cupped hand. Relax → flex. Use only after transverse ligament integrity confirmed (Sharp-Purser negative).
⭐ AA note: Rotation is the primary motion at C1-2. Lateral flexion at AA is minimal. Transverse ligament MUST be screened (Sharp-Purser test) before any AA MET technique. Positive Sharp-Purser = absolute contraindication.

Cervical Spine — Joint-Level (C2–C7 Segmental)
Segment / DysfunctionTechniqueDirection / BarrierPosition / Setup
C2–C4 Rotation Restriction Joint MET Rotation + ipsilateral SB to barrier; resist return to midline Supine; localise level with palpation, take to 3-plane barrier (flex/ext + SB + rot). Client rotates back toward midline against hand. Relax → take further into barrier. 3–5 reps.
C4–C7 Extension Restriction (common) Joint MET PIR Extension + ipsilateral SB + rotation; resist flexion Supine; lower cervical extended to barrier. Client flexes chin toward chest against clinician's hand. Relax → extend further. Common in forward head posture.

VBI screen before any cervical technique: sustained rotation for 10–15 sec each direction, watch for dizziness, nystagmus, diplopia, dysarthria.

Lordotic Curve — Hyperlordosis / Lumbar Extension Pattern
TargetTechniqueDirectionPosition / Setup
Iliopsoas (primary driver of hyperlordosis) PIR RI Hip extension (lengthen hip flexor); resist hip flexion (PIR) or activate glute (RI) Thomas test: supine, one leg off table. PIR: client flexes hip against hand × 7 sec. RI: client squeezes glute/extends hip. Both → posterior pelvic tilt + reduce lordosis.
Rectus Femoris PIR Knee flexion + hip extension; resist knee extension Prone; knee flexed to barrier. Client extends knee against clinician's hand. Relax → flex further. Distinguishes from iliopsoas by knee position.
Lumbar Erector Spinae (overactive) PIR Lumbar flexion; resist extension Supine, knees to chest. Client pushes low back into table (extends) against clinician hold. Relax → curl knees in further. Posterior pelvic tilt re-education during relaxation phase.
Thoracolumbar Fascia / Deep Multifidus PIR Lumbar rotation + flexion; resist extension + rotation back Sidelying; top hip and knee forward (lumbar rotation barrier). Client tries to extend/rotate back against clinician's hand on pelvis. Relax → increase rotation.
Anterior Pelvic Tilt Correction (functional) RI Posterior pelvic tilt via antagonist activation Supine, hook-lying. Client performs abdominal hollowing + posterior pelvic tilt. Clinician provides resistance to hip flexion while client activates lower abs. Goal: retrain neutral pelvis over repeated sets.
⭐ Hyperlordosis sequence: Release iliopsoas first (dominant driver) → then rectus femoris → then lumbar erectors. Follow with motor re-education (posterior pelvic tilt, transversus activation). MET alone without functional retraining has poor long-term results.
Pelvis & SI Joint
Area / DysfunctionTechniqueProtocolNotes
Anterior Iliac Rotation Joint MET Supine; flex hip + knee to 90°. Client pushes knee into clinician's hand (hip flexion). Resist → posterior rotation of ilium. 3–5 reps; reassess with standing flexion test (Gillet). Most common SI dysfunction.
Posterior Iliac Rotation Joint MET Prone; hip extended slightly. Client pushes foot toward ceiling (hip ext). Resist → anterior rotation of ilium. Less common; usually follows a fall on the posterior iliac crest.
Pubic Symphysis Joint MET Supine, knees bent, feet flat. Adductor squeeze against fist (adduction) then abductor push against clinician's hands (abduction). Alternate 3× each. Common postpartum — gentle variation; use feather-edge barrier.
⭐ Postpartum note: Pubic symphysis MET and gentle anterior iliac correction are safe and effective postpartum (6+ weeks cleared). Always confirm obstetrical clearance and use minimal force — 10–15% effort only.

🔴 Contraindications & Precautions

When NOT to use MET — or when to modify

⛔ Absolute Contraindications — Do Not Perform
  • Acute fracture or suspected fracture at the treatment region
  • Active joint dislocation or unstable joint
  • Severe osteoporosis (high fracture risk with any loading)
  • Malignancy in or near the region being treated
  • Active infection, osteomyelitis, or joint sepsis
  • Spinal cord injury with upper motor neuron signs — risk of neurological deterioration
  • Severe ligamentous instability (e.g., alar ligament laxity — use extreme caution at OA joint)
⚠️ Relative Precautions — Modify Technique
Acute inflammation / recent injuryUse RI only; feather-edge barrier; minimal force (10%); avoid PIR directly on acutely injured muscle
Hypermobility (EDS, generalised)Avoid end-range work; use mid-range only; avoid PFS and isolytic; strengthen focus over stretching
Pregnancy (first trimester)Avoid prone; use sidelying; gentle effort only; avoid abdominal pressure; pubic symphysis: minimal force
Postpartum (early)Pelvic MET: wait 6+ weeks; use 10–15% effort; monitor diastasis recti and PGP before loading
Severe muscle guarding / spasmDon't force to barrier; use RI first to reduce tone before PIR; heat + breathing help pre-inhibit
Neural tension / neurological symptomsAvoid techniques that load the neural container; modify position to remove neural tension first
MET vs. Other Techniques — When to Choose
MET over HVLA manipulationCI to manipulation, client preference, acute, high irritability, neurological risk — MET is the safe alternative
MET over passive stretchingChronic hypertonic muscle, failed stretching, protective spasm — PIR achieves gains passive stretch cannot
MET over STR / massageJoint restriction with muscle component, motor control needed, functional carry-over required
Combine with:Joint mobilisation (pre or post), Pilates motor control, home exercise program, STR, dry needling
💡 Best results: MET works best when combined with motor re-education (teaching the nervous system to accept the new range) and a targeted home stretch/activation program. The gain disappears without reinforcement.
